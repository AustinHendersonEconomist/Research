A guided descent · Theravāda meditation

The eight
jhānas

Jhāna (Sanskrit dhyāna) names a series of deeply absorbed meditative states described in the earliest Buddhist texts. Each is defined less by what appears than by what falls away. Step through them below: the field shows an impression of each state, and the panel shows which qualities of mind remain.

Factors present

What remains at each depth

The classical analysis lists five factors in the first jhāna. Each later jhāna is reached by letting the coarsest remaining factor go. The four formless attainments keep the stillness of the fourth jhāna and change only the object.

The light

Many practitioners report a nimitta, a "sign" that appears as concentration deepens. Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga (5th century) says it can look like a star, a cluster of gems, a wreath of flowers or a puff of cotton, and that it differs from person to person. Some lineages make a stable visual nimitta a requirement for jhāna. Others treat it as optional and enter jhāna through a felt sense of pleasure in the body.

How deep is "deep"?

Teachers disagree about the depth the word describes. "Sutta-style" or lighter jhānas allow faint awareness of the body and surroundings. "Visuddhimagga-style" jhānas are so absorbed that sound and bodily sensation drop away. The factor sequence on this page is shared across both readings. What differs is how complete each step must be.

What scanners see

Researchers at Massachusetts General Hospital imaged one highly experienced meditator across 27 jhāna sessions in a 7-tesla scanner. Brain activity shifted in cortical, subcortical, brainstem and cerebellar regions, and tracked the meditator's own ratings of attention, joy, equanimity and sense of self. A 2013 single-subject study had found reward-system activity during the joy of early jhānas. These are single-person case studies and cannot establish general mechanisms.
