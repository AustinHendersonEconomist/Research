Safety, law and respect

Poisonous plants

  • Tutu (Coriaria), common in Otago. Its glossy black berry clusters look edible, and the seeds and plant are deadly.
  • Hemlock (Conium) grows on Otago roadsides and resembles elderflower and wild carrot. Look for purple-blotched hairless stems and a mousy smell.
  • Yew (Taxus), planted in gardens, is lethal. Use only Douglas fir or spruce tips you can identify with certainty.
  • Elder leaves, stems and unripe berries are toxic. Use flowers and cooked ripe berries only.
  • Kōwhai seeds, karaka kernels, ngaio and unripe poroporo are all toxic.

Honey

Honey made from honeydew of passion-vine hoppers feeding on tutu can contain tutin, which has caused serious poisonings. You cannot taste, see or smell it. Buy honey from registered beekeepers who meet the MPI tutin standard. Never use wild or untested comb.

The risk is highest in late-season North Island and upper-South Island honey, but buy tested honey wherever it comes from.

Law

  • Brewing and home distilling for personal use are lawful in New Zealand, and NZ Customs charges no excise on them. "Personal use" is not defined, so do not sell spirits. Gifting may also fall outside personal use.
  • Selling any alcohol needs a licence under the Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act 2012, plus a Customs excise licence for manufacture.
  • Native plants on public conservation land need a DOC permit to collect. On council reserves, check the bylaws. On private land, ask the owner.
  • Seaweed harvest falls under Fisheries rules. Buy dried karengo unless you have confirmed the rules for your spot.

Distilling safely

  • Discard the foreshots, which contain methanol and acetone. Make generous heads cuts, especially with pomace and fruit, because pectin raises methanol.
  • Never distil unattended or near open flame. Ethanol vapour ignites.
  • Use food-grade stills made of copper or stainless steel. Avoid lead solder and plastics in hot zones.

Respect

Plants such as horopito, kawakawa and kānuka are taonga with rongoā (medicinal) significance. Harvest lightly: never more than a fraction of a plant, and never the whole plant. Native fruits are bird food. If you want to tell the story of native ingredients, talk with mana whenua: Te Rūnanga o Ōtākou on the Otago Peninsula and Kāti Huirapa Rūnaka ki Puketeraki at Karitāne.

Sparkling ferments

Wild elderflower and gorse sparkling ferments keep fermenting in the bottle. Use pressure-rated bottles (swing-tops, champagne bottles or PET), vent or refrigerate after 3–5 days, and never use thin glass. Exploding bottles injure people every summer.

A note on kāuru

The sweetness of the tī kōuka

For centuries, Kāi Tahu cooked the stems and roots of the cabbage tree for up to two days in large umu tī. Remains of these ovens still survive across Otago and Canterbury. The cooking turned the stored carbohydrate into kāuru, a sugary food that sustained journeys across the Southern Alps. It is one of the most distinctive sources of sweetness in southern New Zealand, and fermenting it is an obvious idea.

This page gives no recipe on purpose. Kāuru is a cultural practice, not an ingredient. Harvesting the root kills the tree. If the idea moves you, start by learning from the people whose taonga it is, and let any project be theirs to shape.

Honey. MPI, Managing tutin contamination in honey; Food Standards Code tutin limit 0.7 mg/kg. Central Otago thyme honey is from Thymus vulgaris, naturalised around Alexandra since the gold rush.

Law. NZ Customs, excise guidance; Customs and Excise Act 2018, Schedule 3 (no excise on alcohol made for personal use); Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act 2012.

Grain. Harraway & Sons, Green Island, Dunedin, milling since 1867; about 80% of its oats are grown in Otago and Southland. Gladfield Malt (Dunsandel, Canterbury) makes mānuka-smoked malt over West Coast mānuka.

Plants. Te Ara: horopito extends to sea level in the southern South Island; kawakawa's natural range covers the North Island and the northern South Island. Kōtukutuku (konini) berries are edible and fruit January–March.

Kāuru. Canterbury Museum, Gardening at the margins; Te Ara, tī kōuka. Wilding conifer control: Otago Regional Council and the Central Otago Wilding Conifer Group run volunteer days.
