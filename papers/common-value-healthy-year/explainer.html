If these were currencies, an arbitrageur could get rich. Take a dollar out of road safety, where it buys health at $516,000 per QALY, and put it into whatever health spending is valued at $36,000. The same dollar would buy about fourteen times as much health. The question this programme answers is how much of that gap is real.

Feature 1 · The theory

A VSL is a stack of healthy years

In the life-cycle model used to derive the value of a statistical life (Rosen 1988; Murphy and Topel 2006), a VSL is the discounted sum of what each remaining year of life is worth to the person.

If quality of life scales the enjoyment of consumption, a condition that Bleichrodt and Quiggin showed is needed for QALYs and cost–benefit analysis to agree, then each of those years is worth the same amount per unit of health. Call that amount v*.

VSLa = v* × DQALEaDQALE is discounted quality-adjusted life expectancy at age a. Willingness to pay for any morbidity improvement is v* times the discounted QALYs it adds. The VSL and the "value of a QALY" are then one parameter applied to different quantities of health.

This gives two tests. If v* computed from the VSL changes with age, or differs from v* computed from willingness to pay for better health, the assumptions fail in an identifiable way, and the data show where.

Feature 2 · The decomposition

Splitting the gap into four parts

Combining the life-cycle result with a budget-constrained public sector gives an exact identity. The log of the observed gap between a road VSL and a health QALY value is the sum of four terms, and each can be estimated.

Feature 3 · The decision rule

Exchange rates between agencies

Each agency's budget has its own shadow price λ: the welfare a marginal dollar produces there, in dollars of private consumption. A project should be funded when its health benefits, valued at v* and any endorsed context premium, plus its other benefits, exceed λ times its cost. Spending is efficient across government only when every λ is equal.

Fund project j in sector s if   v* Σk πk ΔQjk + ΔYj ≥ λs ΔGjFor pure health projects this is a cost-per-QALY threshold of v*/λs. So a health threshold that sits below v* is a sign that the health budget is constrained, not that health is worth less.

Feature 4 · The empirical programme

Four studies, one number each

Each term in the decomposition gets its own study, designed for New Zealand and pre-registered before data collection.

Study 1

National valuation survey

About 3,000 adults, with Māori and Pasifika oversamples. The same people value mortality-risk reductions in five contexts, health improvements, and health states (composite time trade-off). Scope tests and private-versus-tax payment vehicles are built in.

estimates v*, π, M
Study 2

Sector shadow prices

The marginal productivity of health spending, using the population-based funding formula as a source of variation unrelated to need shocks; revealed thresholds from PHARMAC decisions; marginal benefit–cost ratios of funded transport projects.

estimates λ by sector
Study 3

Citizens' panels

Four deliberative panels, including Māori-led and Pasifika-led panels, decide which context premia should count in public appraisal. Only those with supermajority endorsement enter the standard.

legitimates π
Study 4

Re-ranking NZ decisions

About 40 recent appraisals (medicines, road safety, air quality, school ventilation) are re-ranked under the common rule, with all uncertainty propagated. How much health would reallocation buy?

Pre-registered hypotheses

What the data could show

H1

The VSL-implied value per QALY does not vary with age.

H2

Willingness to pay per QALY is the same for avoiding death as for improving health.

H3

Road, workplace and air-pollution deaths carry premia over hospital deaths.

H4

Stated willingness to pay is less than proportional to the size of the risk reduction.

H5

People state lower values when paying through taxes than privately.

H6

The health budget's shadow price exceeds transport's.

H7

Reallocating a fixed total budget under the common rule raises discounted QALYs by at least 10%.

The proposed standard

Three published numbers

Agencies would report health effects in discounted QALYs, by context, and value them with three numbers published centrally. Every judgement stays visible as its own number.

v*
Common value of a healthy year

One national value, estimated from Study 1 and updated with income.

πk
Endorsed context premia

A short list, possibly all equal to one, kept only if the evidence and citizens' panels support them.

λs
Sector shadow prices

How much a dollar is worth in each budget. Large differences point to a budget allocation problem to be solved in the open.

The standard does not ask transport to adopt health's numbers, or health to adopt transport's. It asks both to use the same value of health and to state openly why their circumstances differ.
